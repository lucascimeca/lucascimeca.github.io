Biography

I received my BEng in Artificial Intelligence and Software Engineering from the University of Edinburgh in 2017 with First Class Honors, graduating top of my class and receiving the Howe Prize for the best performance in Artificial Intelligence.

I obtained my PhD from the University of Cambridge, where I worked with Prof. Fumiya Iida on AI and Computer Vision for bio-inspired robotics systems, and during which time I briefly joined the Center for Brains, Minds, and Machines at MIT to work on coding agents.

Afterwards, three postdoctoral fellowships took me from Harvard University and Dana-Farber, where I used machine learning to answer fundamental questions in tumor biology and immunotherapy, to Yoshua Bengio's group at the Mila AI Institute, where I focused on generative AI and probabilistic inference, with applications to language, vision, scientific discovery, and robotics.

Beyond academia, I have worked as a visiting AI research scientist at Naver Corp. where I worked on representation learning, robustness, and generalization, and as a Staff Research Scientist at Sapient Intelligence, solving problems at the intersection of reasoning, language, and vision, and co-developing HRM-Text and Unigen, state-of-the-art models for their size.

I am currently working on something new in AI & Science, more soon!

Research interests

I am interested in the interplay between foundational AI research and its applications to critical domains, from biology to robotics. In recent years, I have seen AI getting increasingly better at innumerable tasks, from coding and math to language, vision, video, and computer use, especially where data is abundant and feedback comes fast. The physical world is a different story: despite brilliant work in AI and robotics, getting AI to work out there is still one of the hardest problems around.

I believe progress in core domains such as continual learning, fast adaptation, latent reasoning, long-range memory, and world modeling will bring about the next generation of AI models, the first to hope to work within complex real-world environments. Importantly, I believe these core advancements will soon enable us to make science faster, more rigorous, and more reproducible, bringing about discoveries previously out of reach.
